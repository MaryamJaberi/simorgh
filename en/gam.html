Four things the research says

These four come from academic work on hundreds of uprisings over the past century. Under each one, the source is named, so you can go and check it yourself.

You are not alone — and this is arithmetic, not consolation

Where saying what you actually think carries a cost, everyone swallows it. Then each person looks around, sees silence, and concludes they are alone. Everyone makes the same mistake at the same time.

The result is that the state looks stronger than it is — including to itself. And that is why collapses always appear “sudden”: nothing had changed, except that people found out what everyone else already thought.

Timur Kuran, Private Truths, Public Lies (1995) — and his paper on the 1989 collapse in Eastern Europe. Charles Kurzman, in The Unthinkable Revolution in Iran (2004), shows that even in 1979 the participants themselves did not think it was possible until the final weeks.

Numbers matter more than intensity

Across 323 uprisings recorded between 1900 and 2006, nonviolent campaigns reached their stated goal roughly twice as often as armed ones.

The reason is not moral, it is practical: a nonviolent movement can include your grandmother. An armed one cannot. The easier it is to join, the larger the crowd; and it is size that decides the outcome.

Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan, Why Civil Resistance Works (2011), NAVCO dataset.

The decisive moment is the moment of defection

No government falls because of a demonstration. It falls when the people who are supposed to hold it up stop holding it up — the conscript, the clerk, the judge, the manager.

And the research says that defection happens far more often in nonviolent movements, because when the crowd is unarmed, that soldier's own family can be standing in it. Once someone is being shot at, they have nowhere left to go.

The same research; Chenoweth stresses that family and neighbourhood ties between protesters and security forces are one of the main mechanisms.

And none of this is a guarantee

This has to be said, or the rest of the page becomes a lie. The success rate of uprisings — nonviolent and armed alike — has fallen over the past decade. Governments learned: digital surveillance, internet shutdowns, infiltration, and simply outlasting people.

The “3.5% rule” is not a law of nature either. Chenoweth herself calls it a descriptive statistic drawn from a historical sample, not a formula — and says organisation, endurance and strategy matter at least as much as headcount.

Erica Chenoweth, later work; and Civil Resistance: What Everyone Needs to Know (2021).

Your card

Below there is a card for each kind of person. Each says what the research says about your position, and then a few concrete things, ordered from lower to higher risk. You do not have to do all of them. You do not have to do any of them.

Shopkeeper, bazaar trader

In the Constitutional Revolution and in 1979, closing the bazaar was one of the two or three things that actually worked. The reason is simple: you cannot arrest a lowered shutter, and you cannot recover revenue that never arrived.

  1. Know which of your neighbours would close alongside you. That knowledge alone is half the work.
  2. Closing an hour early is still closing, and easier to deny.
  3. Start small: one row of shops, not one city. History says the wave starts in a row of shops.
Relative risk: low to moderate

Worker — especially oil, gas, petrochemicals, ports

This should be said without decoration: in 1979, what broke the state was not the street demonstrations, it was the oil strike. The transition research says the same thing — the main lever sits wherever the state's money comes from.

  1. A strike fund. The research says the single biggest determinant of how many days a strike survives is money, not enthusiasm. A small local fund, set up beforehand.
  2. Working to rule before striking: lower risk, and it measures the collective's real strength.
  3. Horizontal contact with the next workplace. A single-factory strike gets broken; a chain of them does not.
Relative risk: high

Teacher

Teachers' guild councils have been the most continuously organised body in Iran over the past decade. And Kadivar's finding matters here: long, organised movements build more durable democracies than short explosive uprisings. You already have structure — most people do not.

  1. You do not need to build anything; find what exists and keep it alive.
  2. Teachers carry moral standing. Attacking a teacher is expensive for a state, and the state knows it.
  3. An experienced teacher staying healthy and out of prison is worth more than a statement.
Relative risk: high

Nurse, doctor, medical staff

A hospital is where violence becomes undeniable. And the transitional-justice literature is clear on one point: what is recorded today determines what justice is possible tomorrow. A file that is not created now cannot be created later.

  1. Recording the precise nature of an injury in the medical file — that is your professional job, and it is what later becomes evidence.
  2. Not handing over patient information beyond what the law explicitly compels.
  3. Treating without asking who someone is. That is a professional position, not a political one.
Relative risk: high

Student

Honestly: a student movement alone has never changed a government in Iran. 1999, 2009 and 2022 all had brilliant beginnings and stayed alone. The real value of a campus is signalling — showing everyone else they are not alone.

  1. The circle beyond the campus: workers, teachers, shopkeepers. That is exactly the missing link every previous time.
  2. Take the skill outward: translation, documentation, plain-language teaching for someone with less schooling.
  3. Expulsion removes you from the field. Spend deliberately.
Relative risk: high

Driver — truck, taxi, bus, courier

The truckers' strikes of 2018 and 2019 were among the most effective actions of the past decade, for one structural reason: dispersion. Repressing one factory is easy; repressing ten thousand drivers on ten thousand different roads is not.

  1. Dispersion is your shield. Do not gather into something repressible.
  2. “The vehicle is broken” is an entirely lawful sentence.
  3. You are the intercity information network — do not underrate that role.
Relative risk: moderate

Civil servant

A state does not run on buildings and stamps; it runs on the fact that every morning people turn up and do the work. The civil-resistance literature calls this administrative non-cooperation — and says it is nearly unpunishable, because it looks like ordinary bureaucratic slowness.

  1. Work exactly to the regulation. No office anywhere functions when its own rulebook is followed completely.
  2. Stop volunteering initiative. What is not your formal duty, do not do.
  3. Write nothing that could later be used against anyone.
Relative risk: low

Conscript, police, security forces

This is the most important card on the page and it is written plainly. Every body of research agrees on one thing: the decisive decision in any uprising belongs to whoever is holding the weapon. Not to the street.

And a second point, less often said: defection is almost never heroic or public. In most recorded cases it is quiet — arriving late, aiming high, not seeing, being unwell. Nobody is asking you to be a hero.

  1. Your own family is in that same street. This is research, not a slogan: that tie is what has stayed the hand of security forces in most recorded cases.
  2. International and domestic law agree that a manifestly unlawful order does not relieve you of responsibility. What is recorded today gets read tomorrow.
  3. Absence is also a decision. Leave, illness, transfer.
Relative risk: high — and only you know how high

Someone with money

The transition literature says the defection of economic elites is one of the main signals that a system is ending. But the more practical point is this: in human-rights reporting, the greatest effect per unit of money lies in legal defence and in keeping a prisoner's family afloat — not in publicity.

  1. The cost of a lawyer for someone who has none.
  2. The household expenses of a family whose breadwinner is in prison. This is the simplest and most effective thing available.
  3. Not moving the capital out is itself a decision — and it keeps a local economy standing.
Relative risk: moderate

Someone whose money is tight

Bluntly: you carry the most risk and have the least cushion, and no web page has any business telling you what to do. But read Kadivar's finding: the movements that lasted were the ones with everyday networks of mutual aid, not the ones with only street fervour. This is not the lesser contribution; it is the thing that keeps a movement alive.

  1. Know whose bread is short on your street tonight.
  2. A small local fund — the kind that has always existed in Iran and carries no political name.
  3. Staying, and staying well, is itself a contribution. Everyone who is here tomorrow is part of tomorrow.
Relative risk: low

Someone who finds reading and writing hard

This card usually goes unwritten in documents like this, and it should be written. Word of mouth is the oldest medium and the only one that cannot be searched. No system can archive a sentence spoken in a courtyard.

  1. Describe what you saw with your own eyes accurately — no more, no less. Accuracy is what builds credibility.
  2. Do not repeat what you know to be false. Not spreading something is also an action.
  3. Ask someone you trust to read it aloud to you. This page has a “read aloud” button.
Relative risk: low

A woman at home

The home is where that hiding of opinion breaks first. Kadivar's research on 2022 shows it with data: one of the most lasting changes was a shift in bargaining power inside the household itself — not on the street.

  1. Saying what you actually think at the dinner table. This is precisely the mechanism, not a metaphor.
  2. A child who has heard the truth at home recognises a lie outside it.
  3. The women of a neighbourhood are a network. They always have been.
Relative risk: low

Clergy, mosque, religious gathering

This card is also usually cut, and should not be. Historically, clerical dissent has cost a religious state more than any external criticism, because it strikes at its legitimacy on its own ground.

  1. Withholding endorsement. Silence from a pulpit is heard loudly.
  2. Reading the funeral prayer for someone the state would rather went unmourned.
  3. Saying that religion does not belong to a government — which is a religious statement, not a political one.
Relative risk: high

Lawyer, jurist

The transitional-justice literature — Elster, Hayner, Teitel — insists on one point: the files built today set the ceiling of tomorrow's justice. Whatever is not recorded cannot later be proven, whatever government arrives.

  1. Record, even when the case is lost. The document is for later.
  2. Free representation for someone who has no one.
  3. Teaching ordinary people the plain basics of detention rights — low risk and very effective.
Relative risk: high

Artist, writer, musician

If the problem is that everyone believes they are alone, then what breaks it is an undeniable public signal — something everyone sees at once and knows that everyone else has also seen. A song does exactly that, which is why it is more dangerous than a statement.

  1. Make something that can be understood without explanation — an image, a tune, a short line.
  2. Simple and repeatable: something a grandmother can also repeat.
  3. It can be made anonymously. The work belongs to itself.
Relative risk: high

Émigré, diaspora

This has to be written slightly uncomfortably: the research says outside pressure rarely produces change and can delegitimise the movement inside. But the same research says the diaspora is genuinely effective at a few specific things — and deciding on behalf of people inside is not one of them.

  1. Documentation and archiving. What is preserved out here is deleted in there.
  2. Money reaching a prisoner's family — the most measured thing that can be done from outside.
  3. Technical infrastructure: circumvention, mirrors, keeping a channel open.
  4. And one of the hardest: not prescribing for the person who is paying the cost.
Relative risk: low

Professional, academic, specialist

Homa Katouzian calls Iran a “short-term society”: each era starts from zero because institutional knowledge is never transmitted. Which means your step may be the most boring thing available — and also the most durable.

  1. Write down how a ministry, a hospital, a power grid actually works.
  2. Keep figures and documents somewhere that does not disappear with one person.
  3. Translate this very literature into plain Persian — most of these books have never been translated.
Relative risk: low

Someone who does not want to, or is afraid

This card is serious, not a courtesy. The same 3.5% rule means 96.5% did not take part — and those uprisings still reached their goal. Not wanting to is not betrayal, and fear is rational when people are genuinely being executed.

  1. Doing nothing is a legitimate choice and this page respects it.
  2. Not doing the harmful thing is itself an action: not reporting, not repeating, not signing.
  3. Staying alive, employed and upright is your share in any tomorrow.
Relative risk: low

Risk — do not skip this

This page does not know your situation

People are being executed in Iran right now. UN reporting describes death sentences continuing to be carried out under the cover of war. This is not a theoretical discussion.

  • Every figure you read above is an average across 323 cases over a century. You are one person on one street. An average does not decide for you.
  • A step that removes you from the field is not a step. In all of this research, what produces results is endurance, not one large gesture.
  • In most recorded cases, what harmed people was not courage but a phone.
  • This page deliberately offers no technical security advice. Bad security advice is more dangerous than none. For that, go to the specialists — the Access Now Digital Security Helpline, or the EFF's Surveillance Self-Defense guide.
  • Nobody — not this site, not any channel, not anyone abroad — has the standing to decide what risk you should accept.

People who disagree with this page

If we only presented the supporting case, this would be advocacy rather than analysis. These are the most serious academic objections to what you just read.

“Regimes born of revolution die slowly”

Systems that emerged from a genuine social revolution — with their own party and army, and elites whose fates are tied together — are unusually durable. The IRGC is not the Shah's army. This objection says comparing Iran today with Eastern Europe in 1989 is a category error.

Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way, Revolution and Dictatorship (2022)

“The most dangerous point is halfway”

The highest probability of civil war lies neither in full autocracy nor in established democracy, but in the middle — which is exactly where every transition passes through. Any cost estimate that fails to price this in is an underestimate.

Barbara Walter, How Civil Wars Start (2022)

“Remember Iraq”

In 2003, dissolving the army and the party apparatus at a stroke did not cleanse the state or produce democracy; it destroyed the state. Any plan that begins with “dissolve the IRGC” has to explain why the result is not Iraq in 2003.

Toby Dodge, Iraq: From War to a New Authoritarianism (2012); and Libya after 2011

“Iran does not accumulate institutions”

The “short-term society”: each era in Iran erases its predecessor's legacy and starts from zero. If that is right, merely changing the government guarantees nothing.

Homa Katouzian

“And the success rate has fallen”

The researchers in this field say themselves that over the past decade the success rate of uprisings of every kind has declined, because governments have learned. The “3.5% rule” is a historical observation, not a law of physics.

Erica Chenoweth, later work

The smallest possible step

If only one thing survives this whole page, let it be this: the thing that opens the lock in that research is not large. One true sentence, to one person who trusts you.

That is not a metaphor; it is literally the mechanism. What makes a state look larger than it is, is that each person believes they are alone. And the only thing that breaks it is one person finding out they are not.

The rest — numbers, time, organisation — comes after that.
