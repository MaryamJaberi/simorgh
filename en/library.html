How do we know?

Everything claimed in this collection comes from somewhere. This page lists those places — grouped by which claim each one is attached to, not alphabetically. The ones marked in red contradict us; they are deliberately shelved next to the rest.

The three marks

What they mean

  • Core — if you only read a few, read these.
  • Against us — this source weakens or refutes the claim of this collection.
  • Data — a dataset or a numerical report, not a book.

One warning

  • Most of these have never been translated into Persian. That is itself one of the problems.
  • The note under each entry is a guide to choosing, not a summary of the book.
  • None of these rules on Iran today. They are tools for thinking, not verdicts.

Why governments fall suddenly

The theoretical core of this collection's central claim: everyone knows, nobody says it, until the moment everyone says it at once.

Private Truths, Public Lies Timur Kuran · 1995
Core
The theory of preference falsification. What this collection describes intuitively is given its precise form here. If one book is to be added, it is this one.
“Now Out of Never” Timur Kuran · paper · 1991
Core
Why nobody saw the 1989 collapse in Eastern Europe coming — not the intelligence services, not the academy, not the protesters themselves. It is short, and it is the best scientific answer to the entire genre of “I will tell you the date of the fall”.
The Unthinkable Revolution in Iran Charles Kurzman · 2004
Core
The same argument, but about 1979 itself: until the final weeks, even the people in the streets did not think it was possible. The most direct reply to anyone offering a precise date.
“The Dynamics of Informational Cascades” Susanne Lohmann · paper · 1994
The Monday demonstrations in Leipzig as an informational cascade — the quantitative model of the thing we describe with the metaphor of a table.
The Logic of Collective Action Mancur Olson · 1965
The free-rider problem: why a dissatisfied majority does not necessarily move. The basis of any serious discussion of “why don't people rise”.

Game theory and the structure of power

This collection presents itself as “a proposal in the form of game theory”. These are the standard works behind that claim.

The Dictator's Handbook Bueno de Mesquita & Smith · 2011
Core
Selectorate theory: a ruler pays off the small essential group, not the people. It explains directly why “the roles” and “where you sit at the table” is the right question. The academic version is The Logic of Political Survival (2003).
The Politics of Authoritarian Rule Milan Svolik · 2012
Core
Every dictator's two permanent problems: controlling the population, and controlling the insiders. Given the present situation — a leadership council, a weakened centre, internal rivalry — this is the most current book on the list.
How Dictatorships Work Geddes, Wright, Frantz · 2018
Data
Data-driven: how authoritarian regimes since 1946 have actually ended — coup, election, uprising, foreign pressure — and what each route led to. The dataset is public.
Economic Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy Acemoglu & Robinson · 2006
The credible-commitment problem: why elites do not hand over power even when it would be in their interest. The core of any negotiated transition. The Narrow Corridor (2019) is the general-reader version.
The Strategy of Conflict Thomas Schelling · 1960
Bargaining, credible threats, focal points. If this collection really is “game theory”, it is incomplete without Schelling.
Revolution and Dictatorship Levitsky & Way · 2022
Against us
This book refutes our claim. It argues that regimes born of social revolution — with their own party and army and elites bound to a common fate — are unusually durable. Which means comparing Iran with Eastern Europe in 1989 may be fundamentally wrong.

Transition: getting from here to there

Transitions from Authoritarian Rule O'Donnell & Schmitter · 1986
Core
The hardliner/softliner frame on both sides, and the logic of the pact. The spine of any analysis of transition — and effectively the map that our “table” reconstructs.
Why Civil Resistance Works Chenoweth & Stephan · 2011
Core
The NAVCO data and the “3.5% rule”: 323 uprisings between 1900 and 2006; nonviolent 53%, armed 26%. The main empirical source behind “Your Card”.
Civil Resistance: What Everyone Needs to Know Erica Chenoweth · 2021
Core
The shorter, more current version — and more importantly, the author's own explanation of why the success rate has fallen over the past decade. Anyone quoting only “3.5%” has not read this.
Popular Politics and the Path to Durable Democracy Mohammad Ali Kadivar · 2022
Core
The key finding: long, organised movements build more durable democracies than short explosive uprisings. The author is Iranian, and wrote in MERIP in June 2026 specifically about post-war Iran.
Problems of Democratic Transition and Consolidation Linz & Stepan · 1996
How transition from “sultanism” differs from transition from military or single-party rule — and why sultanism is the worst case for a soft transition.
Democracy and the Market Adam Przeworski · 1991
Democracy as institutionalised uncertainty. For understanding why, in a referendum, the loser accepts the result at all.
The Third Wave Samuel Huntington · 1991
The classic typology: transformation from above, replacement from below, or transplacement. This collection's “three paths” map directly onto it.
From Dictatorship to Democracy Gene Sharp
Its Persian translation has circulated hand to hand for years and is effectively the shared vocabulary of part of the audience. The most operational text on this list.

Iran: history, structure, the IRGC

Iran Between Two Revolutions Ervand Abrahamian · 1982
Core
The baseline reference. Without it, the historical part of any analysis of Iran is standing on air. A History of Modern Iran (2008) is the shorter version.
Democracy in Iran: Why It Failed and How It Might Succeed Misagh Parsa · 2016
Core
Exactly this collection's question, by comparative sociology and independently of us. Its conclusion — that reform from within is a dead end — runs the same way as ours, but the argument is stronger.
A Social Revolution Kevan Harris · 2017
Core
The welfare state, the new middle class, the support networks. Of everything on this list, the most useful for understanding south Tehran — it shows that loyalties were never as simple as assumed.
Vanguard of the Imam Afshon Ostovar · 2016
The institutional history of the IRGC; the standard English-language reference. If the IRGC is the main player at the table, this book documents that claim.
Iran Reframed Narges Bajoghli · 2019
Core
An ethnography of the IRGC's own media producers — how they build narrative and what they are afraid of. Unmatched for the character book, because it shows the player from inside rather than outside.
Captive Society Saeid Golkar · 2015
The Basij as a mechanism of social control — not a military force but a neighbourhood network.
Tortured Confessions Ervand Abrahamian · 1999
The history of the forced confession in Iran. It connects directly to the case of Rouhollah Zam and to every comparable case.
The Iranian Constitutional Revolution, 1906–1911 Janet Afary · 1996
The root of the “mashruteh” this collection keeps invoking — with all its contradictions, rather than its idealised version.
“The short-term society” Homa Katouzian
Against us
A deep objection to this collection's optimism. If each era in Iran erases its predecessor's legacy and starts from zero, then merely changing the government guarantees nothing.
What Iranians Want Arash Azizi · 2024
A close account of “Woman, Life, Freedom” — a bridge between academic history and the present.

After the fall — the most urgent part

Given the present situation this is no longer the “forward-looking” section. Most of the sources here say the main danger comes after the fall, not before it.

How Civil Wars Start Barbara Walter · 2022
Against us
The most dangerous condition is neither full autocracy nor established democracy, but the middle — exactly where every transition passes. Any “cost estimate” that fails to account for this is an underestimate.
Iraq: From War to a New Authoritarianism Toby Dodge · 2012
Against us
Iraq 2003: dissolving the army and the party apparatus at a stroke did not cleanse the state, it destroyed it. A direct warning to any plan that begins with “dissolve the IRGC”.
The Burning Shores Frederic Wehrey · 2018
Against us
Libya after 2011: the top falls with no institutional successor. The nearest scenario to “the centre collapses but nothing is ready”.
Closing the Books Jon Elster · 2004
Core
Transitional justice in historical context: who is tried, who is amnestied, and why the wrong choice at this stage burns the following era.
Unspeakable Truths Priscilla Hayner
Truth commissions: what they can do and — more importantly — what they cannot.
Juan Carlos: Steering Spain from Dictatorship to Democracy Paul Preston
The one real case of “monarchy as a bridge to transition”. Since this collection invokes the constitutional tradition, this case should be read both as evidence and with all of its limits.
Chile's 1988 plebiscite the “No” campaign
The operational model of a referendum held under an authoritarian government rather than after one. The closest historical analogue to what this collection proposes.

Human rights, media and data

UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran OHCHR
Data
The official, citable reference in any policy setting. The mandate was extended in January 2026. When you are speaking to a European institution, this document carries weight; a media report does not.
Transnational Repression Freedom House · annual report
Data
Transnational repression — precisely the argument that tells a European audience “this is your own problem, not charity”.
ICCT The Hague · Crisis Group · ECFR
Data
Short policy analysis in the register a Brussels audience reads. ICCT is the source used in the Europe talk.
Amnesty · HRW · Iran Human Rights · Boroumand Center · HRANA
Data
Case documentation and execution figures. For any numerical claim about repression, the source should be one of these rather than a channel.
NetBlocks · IODA · Access Now
Data
Internet shutdowns, in near real time and chartable. Access Now also runs a digital security helpline.
V-Dem · NAVCO · ACLED · UCDP
Data
Democracy indices, nonviolent campaigns, and protest and conflict data. The inputs to any cost estimate that intends to be serious.
Twitter and Tear Gas Zeynep Tufekci · 2017
Why networked movements grow fast and break fast. The theoretical explanation of why AmadNews was effective and why it was not enough.
MERIP · Amwaj.media · Bourse & Bazaar · Iran Primer
Specialist, current analysis. For daily monitoring, read the Persian-language outlets alongside a note on their ownership and editorial line — which is itself part of reading them properly.

Myth and literature

The narrative layer of this collection has sources too.

The Conference of the Birds Attar of Nishapur
Core
The Simorgh itself comes from here — thirty birds who set out and discover at the end that the thing they were seeking was themselves. The central metaphor of this entire collection.
Shahnameh Ferdowsi · Khaleghi-Motlagh edition
Core
The source of every symbol used here — Zahhak, Kaveh, Fereydun, the Simorgh. Dick Davis's English translation for non-Persian readers.
Kelidar · The Colonel Mahmoud Dowlatabadi
Political history from below, in the voices of ordinary people. The closest available model to what the novel in this collection does.
Savushun Simin Daneshvar
Occupation and collapse seen by a woman, from inside a house — the angle that usually gets cut.
Prince Ehtejab Houshang Golshiri
The disintegration of a family as the disintegration of an order.
Encyclopaedia Iranica
Data
The precise reference for mythology — for when you want to be sure a symbol has been used correctly.

And one last piece of honesty

What this list is not

  • It is not complete. No list is.
  • It is not neutral — it is arranged around this collection's claims, not around the whole of Iranian studies.
  • It is not a substitute for reading the sources. The notes are a guide to choosing.

What is deliberately absent

  • Channels that “predict the collapse”. No claim that supplies a date and cannot be verified has entered this list.
  • Any figure without a citable source.
  • Any quotation attributed to a real person without documentation.
