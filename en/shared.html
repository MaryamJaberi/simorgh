Iranians and Israelis

Forty-seven against two thousand six hundred

The official enmity between Iran and Israel is forty-seven years old. Their relationship is about two thousand six hundred. This page is about the other 2,553 years — and about why that arithmetic matters to an Iranian today.

First, the arithmetic

The enmity's share of the whole history.
This is not a metaphor, it is a division: forty-seven divided by two thousand six hundred. That single figure says enmity is not a natural condition but a political decision — and decisions have start dates.

And then, where we actually stand

This page does not pretend the war away

Since February 2026 a war has been under way in which Iran came under American and Israeli attack. A ceasefire was agreed in April 2026 and extended in June, but reporting says both sides have violated it repeatedly and the Strait of Hormuz has remained effectively closed. Cities have been damaged, people displaced, and executions have continued under the cover of the war.

So, without decoration: this page does not say “we are really friends”. Today we are not. What it says is that the ordinary person in neither country chose this war, and that what existed before this enmity is larger than the enmity itself. If someone has lost their home, this page is not asking them to forgive anything.

What is actually shared

Five things, each with a source. Where we had no figure we wrote none — and in one place we rejected a well-known figure, because the primary source does not support it.

The oldest thread

Around 537 BC, Cyrus permitted the Jews exiled in Babylon to return and rebuild the Temple. In the Hebrew Bible, Cyrus is the only non-Jew called “messiah” — that is, anointed. The Cyrus Cylinder is in the British Museum today.

A necessary caveat: the Cyrus Cylinder is not “the first charter of human rights.” That reading is largely a construction of the 1950s onward; the text follows the standard form of Mesopotamian royal inscriptions, and the British Museum says as much. The thread is real; this particular title is not.

The Book of Esther is set in the Achaemenid court at Susa, and the festival of Purim, still kept every year, comes from that account. The tomb attributed to Esther and Mordechai stands in Hamadan and was a Jewish pilgrimage site for centuries.

Even in the language: Old Persian words survive in the Hebrew text of the Bible — among them pardes (which became “paradise”) and dat, meaning law. These are the loanwords of a long neighbouring, not a coincidence.

And the strongest fact in this thread is the least often heard: the Babylonian Talmud — the foundational text of rabbinic Judaism after the Torah — was written on Sasanian soil. It carries Middle Persian loanwords and traces of Sasanian law. Judaism as practised today was, in part, shaped in Iran.

Cyrus Cylinder: the British Museum. The texts of Esther and Ezra. For mythology and etymology, Encyclopaedia Iranica.

A living community, not a memory

The Jews of Iran are one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities in the world — roughly 2,600 years, from the Assyrian and Babylonian captivities.

Before 1979, close to 100,000 Jews lived in Iran. Estimates today put it at 8,500 to 9,100 — still the largest Jewish community in the Middle East outside Israel. One seat in the Majlis is reserved for a Jewish representative.

And the other side of the same coin: 200,000 to 250,000 Israelis have Iranian ancestry, whole or partial. A significant number of Israelis have a grandparent who spoke Persian. These two populations are not strangers to each other; they are relatives.

Population figures: the English Wikipedia entry “Iranian Jews” and the World Jewish Congress. These are ranges, not exact counts.

A small chapter of the world war — and one correction

Abdol Hossein Sardari, Iran's chargé d'affaires in occupied Paris, worked between 1940 and 1944 to keep Jews of Iranian origin in France outside the scope of Nazi anti-Jewish law. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum calls him “an Iranian hero of the Holocaust”.

But here we have to apply to one of our own favourite stories the same rule we apply everywhere else in this collection. It is widely repeated that Sardari issued “1,500 passports”. The Museum does not confirm that figure and calls it “implausibly high”. What is documented is a list of 91 people in May 1943 — and that most of that community survived the occupation.

The smaller documented number is better than the larger undocumented one. What Sardari did for 91 people was still a large thing.

United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, entry on Abdol Hossein Sardari — including its explicit rejection of the figure 1,500.

The thirty years nobody remembers

Iran voted against the partition of Palestine in 1947. But on 6 March 1950 it recognised Israel de facto. What followed looks strange today:

Oil sales from 1957; after the Suez Canal closed in 1967, the joint construction of the Eilat–Ashkelon pipeline; direct El Al flights to Tehran; cooperation in agriculture, medicine, water and road building; an Israeli school in Tehran; and exports that rose from $22.3 million in 1970 to $103.2 million in 1977.

And then its ending, which has a precise date: six days after Khomeini's return, the Israeli mission compound was handed to Yasser Arafat and the street was renamed Palestine. The enmity has a birthday. Something with a date of birth can also have a date of death.

Encyclopaedia Iranica, entry “ISRAEL i. Relations with Iran”.

And the resemblances nobody counts on

Both peoples are non-Arab in a largely Arab region; both have a pre-Islamic identity and a continuous literary tradition; both have enormous diasporas — and in Los Angeles those two diasporas are effectively one neighbourhood; and both societies carry a deep religious–secular fracture inside themselves, not against the outside.

But the most important resemblance for this site is this: both populations have, in recent years, taken to the streets in enormous numbers against their own governments. Iran in 2022; Israel in 2023 against the judicial overhaul, week after week, in what reporting described as hundreds of thousands.

One caveat is required: Israel's 2023 movement was a reform movement, not a revolutionary one — and Chenoweth herself stresses that her data covers maximalist campaigns, so the “3.5% rule” does not transfer to it. The comparison is invalid on that axis. But the simple conclusion stands: in both countries, the ordinary person's principal quarrel is with their own government, not with the people on the other side.

For the 2023 protests: news reporting of the period (“hundreds of thousands”, and over 200,000 at a single event). For the methodological caveat: Erica Chenoweth. More in the Library.

What this page does not say

This section matters more than the one above it. Shared history can very easily be put to work justifying things that cannot be justified.

It does not say the war was right

This page passes no judgement on the rights and wrongs of the 2026 war. Two thousand years of history justifies no bomb and returns no one to life.

It does not say change comes from outside

The opposite. The whole argument of this collection — and the most explicit sentence Kadivar wrote about post-war Iran — is that the answer will not come from outside. No country has arrived at democracy by way of foreign attack, and the transition literature says this with data rather than slogans.

Mohammad Ali Kadivar, MERIP, June 2026

It does not say Iran was always good to Jews

“Almost uninterruptedly positive until 1979” is an overstatement. The Safavid and early Qajar periods include waves of forced conversion, segregated quarters and ritual-impurity law. The correct frame is “better than contemporary Europe,” not “always good.”

It does not resolve the question of Palestine

Millions of Palestinians live under occupation and blockade, and no page about Iranian–Jewish kinship resolves that. Anyone who brings up shared history in order to push it aside is doing propaganda, not history. This page makes no such claim and refuses to be read in its place.

It does not say the Jews of Iran are free

They live under the same government as everyone else. Reporting describes them walking a narrow line during the war: obliged to be Iranian and to stay clear of any accusation of ties to the enemy at the same time. The existence of that Majlis seat is not evidence of freedom.

And it asks nobody to forgive

Historical kinship creates no obligation to forgive. Someone who has lost a person they loved has a right to their own anger. This page only says: send that anger to the right address — and the right address is a government, not a people.

Why this matters for this collection

The Europe talk carries a mandatory sentence that has to survive every revision: “This is about a state — not a religion, and not a community.”

This page is that sentence in a mirror. If we want a European audience to separate the Islamic Republic from the people of Iran, then we have to separate the Israeli government from its people too. A rule applied only in one's own favour is not a rule.

And practically it arrives at the same place as Your Card: an Iranian's step and an Israeli's step both point in the same direction — at their own government.
